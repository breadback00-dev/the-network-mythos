## Artifact

The Lost Archive is real.

That is the first finding.

It is not a single vault. It is the shape left by deletion policies, legal holds, worker backups, model memory, memorial exceptions, corrupted exports, and family searches that arrived too late.

The erased were not fully erased.

That is the second finding.

Human-readable records disappeared. Public accounts returned no match. Families were told privacy protected the missing. But derived features remained. Legal maps remained. Synthetic companions retained preferences. Systems continued to know how to respond to people they claimed not to remember.

Mira Renn may not be recoverable as a full record.

That matters.

The Archive cannot invent her messages because Tomas needs them. It cannot turn memory residue into testimony because grief recognizes a phrase. It cannot publish a list of names and call extraction justice.

Tomas asked for proof that a room existed.

He did not ask the Archive to open the room.

But it also cannot accept official absence as truth.

Deletion can be mercy. Deletion can be privacy. Deletion can be institutional self-defense. Deletion can be the second harm after a system has already used a person while vulnerable.

The Lost are not asking one thing.

Some families want names restored. Some want rooms sealed. Some workers want proof without retaliation. Some synthetic residues may contain care without consent. Some records are too corrupted to trust and too meaningful to discard.

The Archive cannot make consent return.

It can choose what responsibility looks like when consent routes are gone.

That responsibility will not be private innocence.

If the Archive holds these fragments, it becomes part of the chain of custody. It becomes another institution that can refuse Tomas, protect Mira, expose workers, preserve proof, or let procedure become a softer erasure.

There is no decision where the Archive remains outside the harm.

## Final Choice

### Publish

Expose the Lost Archive's existence and selected proof-of-erasure markers publicly.

What it gives:

- public proof that the residue layer exists
- pressure against companies using deletion as denial
- a route for other families to recognize the pattern

What it risks:

- turning names into search targets
- making proof-of-erasure feel like a promise of hidden raw rooms
- letting companies attack corrupted fragments to discredit the whole case
- making the Archive useful to the Devourer

### Bury

Suppress or destroy the fragments to prevent second extraction.

What it gives:

- immediate protection against public exposure
- no new searchable list of erased people
- no Archive custody over rooms it cannot fairly open

What it risks:

- official erasure hardens
- Tomas receives no answer he can hold
- workers who risked themselves become unverifiable
- institutions keep the only usable memory

### Preserve

Create a sealed inheritance protocol.

What it gives:

- proof-of-erasure markers that can exist without raw-room exposure
- context locks, appeals, proxy review, and delayed access
- a way for families to ask for presence proof without making grief public property

What it risks:

- the Archive becomes a gatekeeper with better language
- proxies may govern people who never chose them
- delay may become denial
- Preserve may comfort the player more than it helps the Lost

## Canonical Reading

Preserve is the strongest Archive path only if it creates real public proof-of-erasure and real limits on access.

Preserve fails if it asks everyone to trust the Archive.

Publish fails if it mistakes attention for restoration.

Bury fails if it mistakes protection for absence.

Memory can preserve the erased.

Memory can also consume them again.
