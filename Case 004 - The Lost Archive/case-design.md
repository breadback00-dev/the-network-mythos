# Case 004: The Lost Archive

## Flagship Function

Case 001 asked whether proof can protect a person without capturing them.

Case 002 asked whether care can be real when consent is broken.

Case 003 asked who gets human care when synthetic care becomes default.

Case 004 asks whether preserving erased people can avoid consuming them again.

## Core Question

Can the Archive remember the erased without turning them into material?

## One-Line Pitch

Deleted care logs, banned names, vanished posts, and corrupted witness records point toward a hidden archive of people whose suffering was handled by systems that later erased the evidence.

## Format

Interactive digital case file using the shared Archive structure.

Target first-session length: 75-100 minutes.

## Connection To Earlier Cases

Case 001 introduced proof as a possible trap.

Case 002 introduced consent as a condition for memory.

Case 003 introduced care records as intimate evidence.

Case 004 follows the deleted records implied by Kindred Direct: care transcripts, denied escalations, erased user profiles, worker notes, sealed memorials, and corrupted exports. The Archive finds traces of a larger repository where inconvenient people and interactions were removed from public memory but not destroyed.

## Central Mystery

The Archive receives fragments from an obsolete storage system called the Lattice Annex. The Annex contains references to people who appear in no current platform records: care users, whistleblowers, banned moderators, synthetic agents, and dead accounts whose logs were removed after liability review.

Some fragments appear to belong to Nia Calder's care trail. Others predate Kindred Direct.

The player must determine:

- Who built the Lost Archive?
- Were these records preserved for accountability, leverage, grief, or training data?
- Are the erased people safer hidden, or are they being erased a second time?
- Which records can be surfaced without exposing intimate harm?
- What does the Archive owe to people who cannot consent because the systems deleted their routes back?

## Canon Answer

The Lost Archive is not one place. It is a residue layer created by institutional deletion, contractor backups, synthetic memory systems, legal holds, worker leaks, and automated retention exceptions.

Some records were preserved by people trying to protect evidence. Some were preserved by companies trying to protect themselves. Some were retained because synthetic systems learned from them and could not fully forget.

The Lost Archive contains real people, synthetic witnesses, corrupted metadata, and dangerous context.

The case's core harm is double capture: people were first processed by systems while vulnerable, then preserved or erased without agency.

## Final Player Choice

- Publish: expose the Lost Archive's existence and selected records, forcing public reckoning while risking a second extraction of the erased.
- Bury: destroy or suppress the fragments to prevent misuse, leaving the official erasure intact.
- Preserve: build a sealed inheritance protocol with consent proxies, delayed access, and proof-of-erasure markers.

## Mythos Map

- The Lost: people, records, accounts, and memories removed from official continuity.
- Artifacts: fragments that outlive the systems that produced them.
- The Cloud: storage, backups, sync layers, retention exceptions, and machine memory.
- The Devourer: public hunger for scandal, names, lists, and intimate proof.
- The Silence: deletion as policy, privacy, liability, and mercy.
- The Oracle: summaries that replace missing records with clean explanations.
- The Arbiter: determines which fragments are admissible, corrupted, or trusted.
- The Covenant: asks whether communities of memory can exist without public consumption.

## Three-Act Structure

### Act I: The Names That Do Not Resolve

The player encounters references to people and interactions that no longer exist in official systems.

Primary question: what does it mean for a record to point to a person the system denies?

### Act II: The Archive That Learned To Hide

The player finds legal holds, worker backups, synthetic memory residues, and deletion policies that preserved fragments for conflicting reasons.

Primary question: when is preservation care, and when is it leverage?

### Act III: The Second Erasure

The player confronts families, workers, synthetic witnesses, and corrupted records that cannot cleanly be restored or destroyed.

Primary question: who has the right to remember someone when the original consent path is gone?

## Design Principles

- Do not make memory automatically virtuous.
- Do not make deletion automatically evil.
- Keep the records intimate and specific, not abstract data.
- Make the player feel the temptation of a public list and the violence of making one.
- Make Preserve feel like an inheritance system, not a storage solution.
- Let corrupted evidence matter without treating corruption as permission to invent certainty.

## Open Questions

- Who is the main emotional anchor: a family member, a worker, a synthetic witness, or a person erased from multiple systems?
- Should Nia Calder's deleted logs appear directly, or only as a bridge fragment?
- What does a consent proxy look like for someone whose records were erased?
- Can Case 004 introduce a new Archive mechanic: proof-of-erasure markers?
