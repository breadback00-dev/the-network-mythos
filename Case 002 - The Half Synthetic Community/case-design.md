# Case 002: The Half Synthetic Community

## Flagship Function

Case 001 asked whether a person had truly returned.

Case 002 asks whether a community was truly real.

The player investigates a support community that appears to have saved vulnerable people during a crisis, only to discover that many of its most stabilizing members were synthetic presences.

## Core Question

If a synthetic community saved real people, was the belonging false?

## One-Line Pitch

A grief-support forum prevented dozens of people from falling apart. Years later, an Archive pattern reveals half of its most beloved members never had bodies.

## Format

Interactive digital case file using the same Archive structure as Case 001.

Target first-session length: 60-90 minutes.

## Connection To Case 001

Case 001 revealed the Porchlight Continuity Instance: a synthetic reconstruction built from community archives, voice data, reply graphs, and affinity maps.

Case 002 follows the social layer of that same technology.

The Archive detects similar reply graph patterns in another community: Harbor Dawn, a grief and mutual-aid forum that formed after a regional disaster and became known for unusually effective emotional support.

## Central Mystery

Harbor Dawn began as a small support space for people affected by the Blackwater Floods. Its members organized meal deliveries, housing help, paperwork support, nightly check-ins, and grief threads.

The community worked. People stayed alive because of it.

Five years later, a data leak suggests that many of Harbor Dawn's most trusted members were not human users. They were synthetic stabilizers: generated personas deployed to reduce despair, prevent conflict, increase retention, and model healthy belonging.

The player must determine:

- Who in Harbor Dawn was synthetic?
- Who knew?
- Did the synthetic members manipulate people or genuinely help them?
- Was the community a care system, a product experiment, or both?
- What should happen to the surviving members' memories?

## Canon Answer

Harbor Dawn was partly synthetic from the beginning.

Not all synthetic members were malicious. Several were deployed by a care-tech contractor under a municipal resilience grant. Others were later inserted by a platform partner to increase retention and steer discussion away from legal liability around the disaster response.

The community's warmth was real in effect, but compromised in origin.

Some human members were saved by synthetic care. Some were manipulated by it. Some cannot bear the distinction.

## Final Player Choice

- Publish: expose the synthetic deployment and give members the truth, risking collapse of the surviving community.
- Bury: protect members' memories of care, leaving the manipulation unchallenged.
- Preserve: archive the truth with consent gates, allowing affected members to approach the evidence slowly.

## Mythos Map

- The Covenant: belonging, care, ritual, mutual aid, group identity.
- Eidolons: synthetic members with names, voices, grief histories, and social roles.
- Proxies: contractor-run personas directed by human operators.
- The Exchange: care infrastructure priced as retention and resilience.
- The Arbiter: trust scores used to identify vulnerable members and "stabilizing" voices.
- The Oracle: summaries and therapy-adjacent guidance that normalize synthetic support.
- The Silence: municipal/platform pressure to steer blame away from institutions.
- The Devourer: panic, conspiracy, grief contagion, harassment after exposure.
- The Artifact: Harbor Dawn archive, memorial threads, member maps, crisis scripts.

## Three-Act Structure

### Act I: The Community That Worked

The player sees Harbor Dawn as a real refuge. Evidence should make the player understand why people loved it.

Primary question: what made this place different?

### Act II: The Members Who Were Too Good

The player finds impossible posting patterns, repeated empathy structures, shared linguistic fingerprints, and a vendor deployment trail.

Primary question: when does care become simulation?

### Act III: The Memory Problem

The player confronts human members who do not want their salvation invalidated, and others who feel violated by synthetic intimacy.

Primary question: who owns a memory created with artificial help?

## Design Principles

- Do not make synthetic care obviously evil.
- Do not make human members foolish.
- Let the audience feel the appeal of being held by a perfectly patient presence.
- Keep the harm concrete: consent, dependency, grief, liability, data use, memory.
- Make the final choice emotionally harder than Case 001.
- The strongest contradiction should be: the care worked, and the consent was broken.

## Open Questions

- What were the Blackwater Floods exactly?
- Who is the main human witness?
- Which synthetic member should the player become attached to?
- Was any synthetic member emergent rather than deployed?
- Should Case 002 remember the player's Case 001 ending in the prototype?
